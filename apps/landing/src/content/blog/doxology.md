If you've worshiped with us — on a subway platform, in a park, at Eden or
Love Thy Neighbor — you may have noticed the songs sound a certain way.

Most of them are about God. Not about how we feel about God. Not about what
God has done for us. Just God.

That's on purpose. There's a word for that kind of song, and this post is
about why we build almost everything we sing on it.

One thing first: this is what *we've* chosen to focus on. It isn't a knock on
anyone else's songs. The Bible sings in a lot of registers — grief, thanks,
confession, asking — and we sing those too. We're specializing in the one
that's hardest to find right now.

## The word: doxology

**Doxology** is two Greek words stuck together: *doxa*, **glory** — who God
is, in all His weight — and *logos*, **saying it out loud**. A doxology says
out loud how great God is.

You've sung them your whole life. *"Praise God from whom all blessings
flow."* The angels' *"Holy, holy, holy"* (Isa 6:3; Rev 4:8). Paul, in the
middle of an argument: *"To him be the glory forever"* (Rom 11:36).

What they have in common: **the song is about God, not about us.** A doxology
doesn't report what God did for me, or how I feel about Him. It says who He
*is*, and stops there. You're there to sing it. You're not the subject of it.

## Why we build on it

The Hebrew word for worship, **shâchâh**, means *to bow down*. Jesus says the
Father is looking for worshipers who worship in spirit and truth (Jn 4).
Hebrews 13:15 calls it *a sacrifice of praise*. A bow. An honest one. Given
away.

Which leads to the line this whole catalog rests on:

> If a song never gets past the person singing it, nothing has been given
> away yet.

Grief, thanks, confession, and asking all belong in worship. But each one
carries a risk you don't feel while you're singing. A testimony song has you
dwelling on what God did *for you*. A prayer song, on what *you* still need.
A confession song, on what *you* did. Sung once, at the right moment, that's
holy. Sung every week, they slowly teach a room to spend the whole worship
set thinking about itself — and to walk out having handed God nothing.

People may have cried. The altar is still empty.

We think this has already happened at scale. A lot of the most-sung worship
songs right now put the singer at the center. Make those your staples and you
form people who turn inward the second the music starts.

So we hold two things:

1. **Doxology is the shape where it's hardest for you to end up at the
   center.** When the whole song is about who God is, the giving is built
   into the song.
2. **A catalog needs a strong doxological core to keep its other songs
   honest.** Grief and testimony stay worship when they're sung by people
   whose ears know what pure praise sounds like. Cut off from that, they
   become songs about ourselves with a worship melody underneath.

## The test

For any lyric — ours or a cover — we ask one question: **why is the singer in
this line? Are they giving something to God, or reporting something about
themselves?**

The pronoun doesn't decide it. The job the singer is doing decides it.

### The four shapes we welcome

- **Ascription** — *saying who He is.* Straight declaration of who God is and
  what He's done. This is the center of our catalog. Isa 6:3; Rev 5:12;
  Ps 99; Phil 2:6–11.
- **Offering** — *pointing everything at Him.* Your attention, your wonder,
  your voice, turned Godward: *"Bless the Lord, O my soul"* (Ps 103:1).
  You're in the song, but only as the magnifying glass. God is what you see.
- **Diminishment** — *getting smaller on purpose.* You show up only to shrink
  so the glory looks bigger: *"He must increase, I must decrease."* Isa 6:5;
  Lk 5:8; Jn 3:30.
- **Surrender** — *handing your life over.* You show up only to be given
  away. Rom 12:1; Lk 22:42; Isa 6:8.

### Where good songs drift

Each shape slides toward its nearest cousin, usually in the middle of a line:

- **Saying who He is → telling my story.** *"You are Faithful Father"*
  becomes *"You've never failed me yet."* Now my experience is the proof.
- **Pointing at Him → reaching for me.** *"I lift my hands to bless the
  Lord"* becomes *"I lift my hands to receive."* Same posture, opposite
  direction.
- **Getting smaller → figuring it out.** *"I am nothing without You"* becomes
  *"how did I end up this blessed?"* Now the surprise is the point.
- **Handing over → promising.** *"Have Your way in me"* becomes *"I'll never
  let go."* Now it's my willpower.

Almost nobody does this on purpose. It's just gravity — your own story pulls
the line back home.

## What we sing but don't build on

- **Songs about us** — grief (Ps 13), testimony (Ps 23), confession (Ps 51),
  asking (Ps 40). Their subject is the human experience of God. We sing them.
  We don't build our identity on them.
- **Mixed songs** — a chorus about God wrapped in verses about the singer's
  journey. We'll sing some. But what we're *writing* is God-centered start to
  finish: nothing put on the altar and then taken back off.
- **"Blessed be God" and thank-you songs** (Eph 1:3–14; 1 Pet 1:3–5) — right
  on the line. They pass while the focus stays on what God did, and drift
  when they settle into what we got out of it. Test them line by line.

## Our 80/20 goal

We hold ourselves to a number. In the sets we control, and in everything we
write and release as Public Worship, we aim for **80% about God, 20% about
us.**

The number isn't sacred. The balance is. The 20% keeps grief, testimony,
confession, and asking in the room, where Scripture puts them. The 80% keeps
"we sing those too" from quietly turning into "that's mostly what we sing."

## An invitation

This is the part we care about most.

We want the next wave of worshipers writing songs shaped by what moves God,
not by how a crowd reacts — songs that stay pointed at Him from the first
line to the last, held to Scripture before they're held to the algorithm.

We haven't got this figured out. We expect to keep working it out — in
writing rooms, on platforms, and with anyone willing to hold us to Scripture
while we do. If that's you, we'd love to hear what you write, and we'd love
the conversation even more.

> "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom." (Proverbs 9:10)

We don't start with a method. We start by bowing. We'll get things wrong;
when we do, tell us, with Scripture in hand.

*— The Public Worship Team*

## Further reading

We're working through as many worship songs as we can and we'll publish that
list when it's ready. Until then — read both columns.

**Voices that shaped us:**

- **[A.W. Tozer, *Whatever Happened to
  Worship?*](https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/721486.Whatever_Happened_to_Worship)**
  (sermons preached 1961) — worship as the thing we were made for, and the
  jewel the church misplaced.
- **[Marva Dawn, *Reaching Out without Dumbing
  Down*](https://a.co/d/02CmICyZ)** (1995) — a theology of worship where God
  is both the subject and the audience, and a warning about letting the
  culture around us set the terms.
- **[Bob Kauflin, "Evaluating Worship Song
  Lyrics"](https://worshipmatters.com/2006/12/01/worship-leaders-pastors-evaluating-worship-song-lyrics/)**
  (2006) — a working worship pastor's checklist.

**Voices that check us:**

- **[Isaac Watts, Preface to *Hymns and Spiritual
  Songs*](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/ccel/eee/files/wattsprh.htm)** (1707) —
  the case for worship written in the singer's own voice. A check on
  overcorrecting.
- **[Justin Taylor, "Don't Neglect the Horizontal Dimension of Singing and
  Worship"](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/justin-taylor/dont-neglect-the-horizontal-dimension-of-singing-and-worship/)**
  (TGC, 2017) — the strongest argument against how we've framed this. Read it
  before you trust us too much.
- **[Walter Brueggemann, "The Costly Loss of
  Lament"](https://doi.org/10.1177/030908928601103605)**
  (JSOT, 1986) — why a catalog that skips grief is dangerous in the opposite
  direction.
