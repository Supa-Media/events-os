At Public Worship, we center our catalog on **doxological worship** — songs
whose only content is the glory of God, with the worshiper present only as an
instrument of that declaration.

That's a curatorial focus, not a verdict on anyone else's catalog. Scripture
models many registers — lament, testimony, confession, petition — and we honor
them all. We're specializing in the one we believe contemporary worship most
lacks: the song that simply declares God Himself, from the first line to the
last.

If you're going to lead with us — on a subway platform, in a park, or on a
stage at Eden or Love Thy Neighbor — you don't have to agree with every line
of this. You do have to know what we're aiming at, because it shapes every set
list we'll hand you.

## What doxology means

**Doxology** joins two Greek words: *doxa*, **glory** — the weight and radiance
of who God is — and *logos*, **declaration**. A doxology declares God's glory.

You've sung them your whole life: *"Praise God from whom all blessings
flow."* The angels' *"Holy, holy, holy"* (Isa 6:3; Rev 4:8). Paul,
mid-argument: *"To him be the glory forever"* (Rom 11:36).

The common thread: **the content is God, not us.** A doxology doesn't report
what God did for me, or how I feel about Him. It declares who He *is* — and
stops there. The worshiper may be present, but only as the voice carrying the
declaration, never as its subject.

## The conviction

Worship, in Hebrew, is **shâchâh** — to bow down. Jesus says the Father seeks
worship in spirit and in truth (Jn 4). Hebrews 13:15 calls it *a sacrifice of
praise*. A bow, offered in truth, as a sacrifice: something *given*, laid at
God's feet.

Here's the conviction this catalog rests on:

> A song whose lyrics never leave the worshiper has not yet brought a sacrifice.

Lament, testimony, confession, and petition are all faithful in their place.
But each carries a risk the worshiper rarely sees. A testimony song asks them
to dwell on what God did *for them*; a petition song, on what *they* still
need; a confession song, on what *they* have done. Sung once, in the right
moment — holy. Sung as a steady diet, they quietly train a congregation to
spend its whole worship set thinking about itself, and to walk out having
brought nothing to God at all.

The room may have wept. The altar is still empty.

We believe this drift has already happened at scale: many of the most-sung
songs of the last twenty years put the worshiper at the center, and as the
staple of a church's worship life they form worshipers who turn inward the
moment they begin to sing.

So, two convictions. First: **doxology is the form in which it is hardest
for the self to drift to the center** — when the song's entire content is who
God is, the sacrifice is built into the form. Second: **a catalog needs a
strong doxological core to keep its other songs honest.** Lament and
testimony stay tethered as worship when sung by people whose ears know what
pure declaration sounds like; cut off from that center, they become religious
self-narration with a worship melody underneath.

## The test

For any lyric — ours or a cover — we ask: **what is the function of the
worshiper's presence? Are they giving something to God, or reporting
something about themselves?** The pronoun doesn't decide it. The function
does.

The welcome zone has four shapes — *self appears only to make God greater, or
not at all*. The other five — *self carries the song*.

### The four doxological shapes

- **Ascription** *(pure doxology)*. Direct declaration of who God is and what
  He has done — the center of our catalog. Isa 6:3; Rev 5:12; Ps 99;
  Phil 2:6–11.
- **Offering** *(instrumental doxology)*. The worshiper's attention, wonder,
  and voice turned Godward: *"Bless the Lord, O my soul"* (Ps 103:1). The soul
  is present, but only as the magnifying glass — God is what you see.
- **Diminishment** *(inverse doxology)*. Self appears only to shrink so the
  glory grows: *"He must increase, I must decrease."* Isa 6:5; Lk 5:8; Jn 3:30.
- **Surrender** *(embodied doxology)*. Self appears only to be yielded — the
  worshiper's life itself declaring His lordship. Rom 12:1; Lk 22:42; Isa 6:8.

### Where songs drift

Each shape slides toward its closest anthropological cousin, usually mid-line:

- **Ascription → testimony.** *"You are Faithful Father"* becomes *"You've
  never failed me yet"* — the worshiper's experience as the evidence.
- **Offering → recipient.** *"I lift my hands to bless the Lord"* becomes
  *"I lift my hands to receive"* — same posture, opposite direction.
- **Diminishment → discoverer.** *"I am nothing without You"* becomes *"how
  did I end up this blessed?"* — the surprise becomes the content.
- **Surrender → resolved.** *"Have Your way in me"* becomes *"I'll never let
  go"* — the worshiper's own resolve takes over.

The drift is rarely deliberate — it's the gravity of the worshiper's own
story pulling the line back home.

## What we sing but don't center

- **Anthropological songs** — lament (Ps 13), testimony (Ps 23), confession
  (Ps 51), petition (Ps 40). Their content is the human experience of God. We
  sing them; we don't build our identity on them.
- **Mixed songs** — a doxological chorus wrapped in journey-narrating verses.
  We'll sing some, but the shape we're writing toward is doxological
  *throughout*: the offering starts on the altar and stays there.
- **"Blessed be God" and thanksgiving forms** (Eph 1:3–14; 1 Pet 1:3–5) — on
  the boundary. They pass while the focus stays on God's acts, and drift when
  they dwell on what we received. Test them line by line.

## An invitation

If you write worship music, we invite you into the same standard: the song
that is doxological from the first line to the last, nothing taken back off
the altar. Hold yourself to Scripture before you hold yourself to the
algorithm. We'd be glad to hear what you write.

> "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom." (Proverbs 9:10)

We don't begin with method. We begin with bowing. We will fail; when we do,
tell us, with Scripture in hand.

*— The Public Worship Team*

## Further reading

We're testing as many worship songs as we can and will publish that catalog
when it's ready. Until then, the sources most worth your time:

- **Augustine, *Confessions* X.33** (c. 400) — "more moved by the singing than
  by what is sung… I confess myself to have sinned." Still load-bearing.
- **Isaac Watts, Preface to *Hymns and Spiritual Songs*** (1707) — the defense
  of worship in the worshiper's voice; a check on overcorrecting.
- **Bob Kauflin, "Evaluating Worship Song Lyrics"** (2006) — a working worship
  pastor's checklist.
- **Justin Taylor, "Don't Neglect the Horizontal Dimension of Singing and
  Worship"** (TGC, 2017) — the strongest counter-voice to our framing; read it
  before you trust us too much.
- **Walter Brueggemann, "The Costly Loss of Lament"** (1986) — why a
  vertical-only catalog that skips lament is dangerous in the opposite
  direction.
