A subway platform in Queens on a Tuesday night. A park in the Bronx in July.
The back half of a train car, where nobody chose to be at a worship service and
half the people can't move away from you.

That's where most of our sets happen. And it changes the first question we ask
about a song. In a sanctuary you can assume the room. Out here you can't assume
anything — not the theology, not the language, not whether the person six feet
away has ever heard the name of Jesus said with affection. Whatever we sing has
to be true without a preamble.

So before you lead with us — on a platform, or on a stage at Eden or Love Thy
Neighbor — here's how we choose what we sing, and why.

## Who this is for, and what it isn't

This is written for worship leaders and songwriters we're inviting into the
work. It's a description of one catalog's center of gravity, not a verdict on
anyone else's.

We center our catalog on **doxological worship** — songs whose only content is
the glory of God, with the worshiper present only as an instrument of that
declaration.

That's a curatorial focus, not a judgment. Scripture models many registers —
lament, testimony, confession, petition — and we honor all of them. We're
choosing to specialize in the one we believe is most lacking in contemporary
worship: songs that simply declare God Himself, sustained from the first line
to the last.

If you lead with us, you don't have to agree with every line of this. You do
have to know what we're aiming at, because it will shape the set list you're
handed.

## What doxology actually means

**Doxology** comes from two Greek words: *doxa* (δόξα), meaning **glory** —
weight, radiance, the visible splendor of who God is — and *logos* (λόγος),
meaning **word** or **declaration**. A doxology is, at its simplest, a
declaration of God's glory.

You've heard doxologies your whole life, even if you've never used the word.
*"Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit"* is a
doxology. *"Praise God from whom all blessings flow"* is a doxology. When Paul,
mid-argument in Romans, breaks into *"For from him and through him and to him
are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen"* (Rom 11:36) — that's a
doxology. When the angels cry *"Holy, holy, holy"* (Isa 6:3; Rev 4:8) — that's
a doxology.

The common thread: **the content is God, not us.**

A doxology doesn't report what God did for me. It doesn't describe how I feel
about God. It declares who God *is* — His character, His acts, His worth — and
stops there. The worshiper may be present, but only as the voice carrying the
declaration, never as its subject.

## The conviction underneath it

Worship, in the Hebrew of the Old Testament, is **shâchâh** — to bow down. In
John 4, Jesus says the Father seeks worshipers who worship in spirit and in
truth. In Hebrews 13:15, the writer calls it *a sacrifice of praise — the fruit
of lips that openly profess his name*.

Three anchors: worship is a bow, it's offered in truth, and it's a sacrifice.
Something is *given*. Something leaves the worshiper's hand and is laid at
God's feet.

Here's the conviction this catalog rests on:

> A song whose lyrics never leave the worshiper has not yet brought a sacrifice.

Not every worship song must be doxological. Scripture sings in many registers,
and lament, testimony, confession, and petition are all faithful forms in their
proper place.

But each of those forms carries a risk the worshiper rarely sees. A testimony
song asks them to dwell on what God did *for them*. A petition song asks them
to dwell on what *they* still need. A confession song asks them to dwell on
what *they* have done. Sung once, in the right moment, they're holy. Sung as a
steady diet, they can quietly train a congregation to spend its whole worship
set thinking about itself — its situation, its journey, its longings, its sins
— and to walk out having brought nothing to God at all.

That's the danger we're addressing. It isn't that anthropological songs are
wrong. It's that **their lyrics can, without anyone intending it, leave the
worshiper inside their own perimeter for the entire song** — moved, perhaps
deeply, but still the subject of their own attention. And a worshiper whose
attention never leaves themselves has not yet worshiped.

The room may have wept. The altar is still empty.

Romans 12:1 calls worship a *sacrifice*. A sacrifice is, by definition,
something carried out of the worshiper's possession and laid down in front of
God. If the song never makes it past their own story, no sacrifice has been
offered. There's been feeling — sometimes the deepest feeling of the week — but
nothing has been given.

Look honestly at contemporary worship and we believe this drift has already
happened, at scale. Many of the most-sung songs of the last twenty years are
anthropologically shaped: the worshiper as recipient, discoverer, testifier,
the one who resolves. Each may have served a soul somewhere. Sung as the staple
of a church's worship life, they form worshipers who turn inward the moment
they begin to sing and never make it back out.

Which leads to two convictions:

**Doxology is the form in which it is hardest to inject the self in a way that
becomes unintentional self-idolatry.** When the song's entire content is who
God is, there's no grammatical room left for the singer to drift to the center,
no opening for their story to quietly become the subject, no slot where they
can be exalted while thinking they're exalting God. The sacrifice is built into
the form. Other forms can be worship — but they require a vigilance the
doxological form doesn't, because in them the path back to the self is always
one careless line away.

**A catalog needs a strong doxological core to keep its other songs honest.**
Lament, testimony, petition, and confession stay tethered as *worship* when
they're sung by people whose ears know what pure declaration of God sounds
like. Cut off from that center, they slowly become something quieter and more
dangerous: religious self-narration with a worship melody underneath.

We're not writing against the other registers. We're writing *for* the one we
believe holds them all in their proper orbit — and the one the church most
often lacks.

## The test

For any lyric — one we've written, or one we're considering leading — we ask
two questions:

1. **What is the function of the worshiper's presence?**
2. **Are they giving something to God, or reporting something about
   themselves?**

The pronoun doesn't decide it. The function does.

The welcome zone has four shapes — *self appears only to make God greater, or
not at all*. The not-the-diet zone has five — *self carries the song*.

### The four doxological shapes

Ascription is the core — the purest form. The other three are expressions of
it: ways the worshiper's presence serves the declaration of God's glory rather
than competing with it.

- **Ascription** *(pure doxology)*. Direct declaration of who God is and what
  He has done. The worshiper may be entirely absent from the lyric, or present
  only as "we who declare." This is the center of our catalog.
  Isa 6:3; Rev 4:8; Rev 5:12; Ps 99; Phil 2:6–11; Col 1:15–20.
- **Offering** *(instrumental doxology)*. Self as the giver of attention,
  wonder, voice, awe — the worshiper's faculties turned Godward as instruments
  of the declaration. *"My soul magnifies the Lord."* Self-addressing
  imperatives — *"Bless the Lord, O my soul"* (Ps 103:1), *"Awake, my soul"*
  (Ps 57:8) — are the paradigm: the soul is the addressee, God is the
  destination. The soul is present, but only as the magnifying glass. God is
  what you see. Ps 34:3; Ps 103:1; Ps 121:1; Ps 145:1–2; Lk 1:46.
- **Diminishment** *(inverse doxology)*. Self appears only to be made small so
  that God's glory appears larger. *"He must increase, I must decrease."* The
  worshiper shrinks; the glory grows. Job 40:4; Isa 6:5; Lk 5:8; Gen 18:27;
  Jn 3:30.
- **Surrender** *(embodied doxology)*. Self appears only to be yielded to God's
  authority — the worshiper's life itself becomes a declaration of His
  lordship. Rom 12:1; Lk 22:42; Lk 1:38; Isa 6:8.

### Where songs drift

Each of the four doxological shapes can slide into one of the five
anthropological shapes mid-line or mid-song. The drift is usually toward the
closest cousin:

- **Ascription → testimony.** *"You are Faithful Father, never failing"*
  (declaring God's character) drifts to *"You've never failed me yet"* (the
  worshiper's experience as the evidence).
- **Offering → recipient.** *"I lift my hands to bless the Lord"* (offering
  attention upward) drifts to *"I lift my hands to receive all You have for
  me"* (same posture, opposite direction).
- **Diminishment → discoverer.** *"I am nothing without You"* (being made small
  before God) drifts to *"how did I get this lucky?"* (the worshiper's surprise
  becomes the content).
- **Surrender → resolved.** *"Have Your way in me"* (yielding to God's
  authority) drifts to *"I'll never let go of Your hand"* (the worshiper's own
  resolve about what *they* will do).

The drift is rarely deliberate. It's the gravity of the worshiper's own story
pulling the line back to themselves.

## What we sing but don't center

### Anthropological worship

The songs outside our welcome zone aren't bad — they're **anthropological**
rather than doxological. Their content is the human experience of God rather
than God Himself: lament (Ps 13; Ps 22; Ps 130), testimony (Ps 23; Ps 116; the
Magnificat), confession (Ps 51), petition (Ps 40; the ending of the Lord's
Prayer).

We sing these. We just don't build our identity around them, because the church
already has them in abundance.

### Mixed songs

Many beloved worship songs and hymns have a strong doxological hook — a chorus
that declares God's glory — wrapped in anthropological verses that report the
worshiper's journey to that chorus. They're not bad, and we'll sing some of
them.

But the shape we're trying to write more of is **the song that is doxological
throughout** — no testimony scaffolding leading into a vertical chorus. Just
sustained declaration of who God is. The offering starts on the altar and stays
there. That shape is rare, and its rareness is exactly what we're addressing.

### "Blessed be God" and thanksgiving forms

The biblical eulogies (Eph 1:3–14; 1 Pet 1:3–5; 2 Cor 1:3–4; the Benedictus)
and the thanksgiving forms in Paul's letters sit right on the boundary of our
welcome zone. They pass when their focus stays on God's character and Christ's
acts; they drift when they dwell on what we received ("who comforts us in all
our troubles," "salvation from our enemies").

The form itself is biblical. The drift is what we watch for. Apply the test
line by line.

## How we practice it

- **Theological soundness, non-negotiable.** Every release is checked against
  Scripture and our [Statement of Beliefs](/beliefs).
- **Writers as stewards of the test.** Every writing room has someone whose job
  is to apply the test to the lyric before the song leaves the room.
- **The same standard for songs we didn't write.** When we lead a cover, a
  hymn, or a worship song already in circulation, it goes through the same
  test. Popularity doesn't exempt it.
- **Singability.** Songs are written so a congregation — or a platform full of
  strangers — can actually sing them in the rooms where we lead.
- **Fear of God in the room.** Songwriting that calls itself worship is no
  small thing. We approach the work as people who will give an account.

## An invitation

If you write worship music, we invite you into the same standard. Write the
song that is doxological from the first line to the last — sustained
declaration of God's glory, with nothing taken back off the altar. Hold
yourself to Scripture before you hold yourself to the algorithm.

We'd be glad to hear what you write.

> "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom." (Proverbs 9:10)

We don't begin with method. We begin with bowing. We will fail. When we do, we
want to be told, with Scripture in hand. We're submitting this work to God and
to His people.

*— The Public Worship Team*

## Further reading

We're working through as many worship songs as we can against the test above,
and we'll publish that catalog when it's ready.

In the meantime, these are the free articles and primary sources we've found
most worth the time:

- **Augustine of Hippo, *Confessions* Book X.33** (c. 400). The fourth-century
  locus classicus on music in worship: "When it happens to me to be more moved
  by the singing than by what is sung, I confess myself to have sinned
  criminally." Short, devastating, still load-bearing.
- **Isaac Watts, Preface to *Hymns and Spiritual Songs*** (1707). The
  eighteenth-century defense of writing worship in the worshiper's voice. Read
  it as a check on overcorrecting our own position.
- **Bob Kauflin, "Worship Leaders/Pastors: Evaluating Worship Song Lyrics"**
  (2006). A working worship pastor's checklist for testing lyrics.
  Practitioner-grade.
- **Justin Taylor, "Don't Neglect the Horizontal Dimension of Singing and
  Worship"** (TGC, 2017). The strongest free counter-voice to our framing —
  tests our position against Eph 5:19 and Col 3:16. Read it before you trust
  us too much.
- **Marva Dawn, tribute and quotes** (Christianity Today, 2021). The closest
  free entry point to her critique of self-focused worship: "too much music
  being used today is narcissistic — it's about us and how we feel about God."
- **Walter Brueggemann, "The Costly Loss of Lament"** (summary of the 1986
  essay). The indispensable counter-voice for any catalog tempted to skip
  lament. On his account, a vertical-only worship that excludes "Why are you
  cast down, O my soul?" is theologically dangerous in the opposite direction.
